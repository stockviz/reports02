NephroPlus (Nephrocare Health Services Limited) — Q1 FY27 Analysis

1. Headwinds and Challenges

Structural working capital intensity. Dialysis operates with structurally high working capital because the business deals with government payers, where payment typically arrives over three to four months. Management noted that AR days, while improved from 121 days to 101 days year-on-year on the back of digitization and AI work, are expected to remain relatively high as this is a structural feature of the industry. Expected credit loss provisions run at roughly 2%–2.5% of revenue each quarter under a KPMG-assisted ECL model.

Saudi Arabia investment phase and tender uncertainty. The Saudi market is a tender market and the company is in an investment phase. The first clinic at Riyadh Hospital became operational in July, home dialysis operations have commenced, a medical operator’s license has been obtained, and a response to the Ministry of Health tender RFI has been submitted. However, the formal tendering process was expected to begin in a couple of months, and management explicitly stated these timelines are not fully within their control. Management described the market as binary — win one of four clusters and there is a business; lose and the company exits. The share of loss from the JV was ₹3.6 crore in Q1 FY27 (versus ₹3.1 crore in Q4 FY26). Management declined to guide on the loss trajectory and indicated three to four quarters away from clear visibility, with the tendering process running over the next few quarters. Additional investments would be required depending on tender terms.

Uttarakhand PPP exit. Two clinics under the Uttarakhand PPP were let go because they were value dilutive. Management stated it will not pursue business that does not meet return thresholds.

Other expense pressures. Other expenses rose year-on-year due to three factors: ECL provisions (2%–2.5% of revenue), costs associated with becoming a public company (higher audit fees, legal fees), and upfront investment in business development and foundational activity for international expansion. Sequentially, however, other expenses improved by 133 basis points.

Renal nurse availability. Access to trained renal nurses is a genuine constraint on international expansion in the industry. The NephroPlus International Dialysis Academy (NIDA) was launched as an in-house program to build a pipeline of renal nurses qualified to work anywhere in the world, with the first batch beginning training in Q3.

Clinical AI ambition not yet achieved. The first attempt at predicting adverse events using clinical data and AI algorithms did not reach the confidence level expected. A second phase of that initiative is underway. Management described proactive prediction and prevention of adverse events as the “holy grail” that nobody in the world has done.

Standalone clinic ecosystem in India still maturing. India’s regulatory ecosystem is still coming to terms with standalone clinics; stakeholders are accustomed to the hospital-based model. NephroPlus currently runs 25 standalone clinics in India, and management expects it will take a long time before standalone becomes mainstream.

Capacity constraints limit same-store growth. Dialysis is a fixed-capacity business — only three cycles per machine per day in India, with four-hour mandatory treatment time (four and a half hours including pre and post). At roughly 85% utilization, clinics have little room for volume growth. Management stated this is not a same-store-sales growth story; growth comes from capacity addition.

Price increases are lumpy, not annual. In dialysis, prices typically increase slowly, with occasional lumpy increases after long periods. In the Philippines, a 55%–60% price increase occurred in October 2024 after roughly 10 years. CGHS in India saw a 35% increase after approximately 10–11 years. Management cautioned that the ~11% RPT CAGR should not be expected to continue indefinitely.

Macro and other risks cited in the annual report material. Risks include macroeconomic performance in India and international markets, market competition, execution of company strategy, technological implementations, changes in revenue, income or cash flows, market preferences, exposure to market risks, hiring renal nurses internationally, and non-adherence to recommended treatment sessions or low awareness and financial barriers in the broader industry.

2. Tailwinds and Growth Prospects

Rising CKD burden. The growing prevalence of diabetes and hypertension was expected to lead to a significant increase in chronic kidney disease, with patients eventually progressing to stage five requiring dialysis or transplant.

Shift from unorganized to organized. Roughly 80% of India’s dialysis capacity remains in the unorganized market, with hospitals running their own dialysis operations. Sixteen years ago this was 0%; today the organized market is about 21%–22%. Management expects this shift to play out over the next several years. Hospitals do not generate more than 1% of revenue from dialysis operations, and it is mostly a loss-making business for them because they lack scale and focus. More than 300 private hospitals have partnered with NephroPlus.

Three growth levers. (1) Increasing guest volumes across existing clinics through higher utilization and capacity expansion with additional machines; (2) expanding footprint within existing countries through new clinics and selective small acquisitions; (3) entering new countries and pursuing strategic opportunities including large acquisitions and large PPPs.

International expansion. International operations now contribute around 45% of revenue, up from 30% a year and a half earlier. The company aims to add a new international market every 12 to 18 months. In the Philippines, NephroPlus crossed 50 clinics across 39 cities in six years, becoming the second largest distributed dialysis network, with roughly 6%–7% share of a market of around 900 standalone clinics. In Uzbekistan, NephroPlus is the only private operator, servicing close to 1,800 of approximately 9,000 patients. A subsidiary was incorporated in Kazakhstan on 26 May 2026 to evaluate opportunities across the country and Central Asia.

PPP momentum. The company deepened its footprint in Bihar and signed a new contract in Tamil Nadu during the quarter.

Clinical quality programs. The early vascular access program in Andhra Pradesh across 21 centers and 2,000 guests moved monthly AVF creations from 15% of guests to 30%, reducing mortality to close to 15%. The NephroPlus Index — a composite health score from seven weighted clinical matrices — is live across 29 centers and 2,600 guests, with plans to aggregate to clinical cluster, zone, and country levels. The “blue book” codifies guest-first culture at the last mile.

NIDA and technology. The NephroPlus International Dialysis Academy addresses renal nurse supply structurally. The company is digitizing the guest and clinical experience through guest care and in-center apps, deploying AI across back-office functions, and using RFID to track fixed assets. The reform.ai application uses CCTV and AI algorithms for live auditing of clinical processes such as glove use, with alerts to center, cluster, and quality managers.

Platform moat. Management described four distinct levers creating margin advantage: global procurement of consumables (including contract manufacturing of low-complexity consumables), efficient human resource management through training academies, an in-house biomedical team doing preventive maintenance (built eight years ago to counter information asymmetry with manufacturers), and a lean operating model built in India at the world’s lowest price points.

Asset-light expansion. The company describes its model as asset-light, with hospitals typically providing space and utilities while NephroPlus invests in equipment, furniture, and other assets under a build-own-operate model.

Under-penetration and market growth. India remains in early days of the dialysis market life cycle, with the majority of patients who need dialysis lacking access. The annual report material cites significant under-penetration in Asia with strong market CAGRs (17–23%) and high patient stickiness with recurring revenue.

3. Key Risks

  • Saudi tender outcome risk. Management explicitly stated there is a probability the company may not secure the tender. The market is binary — win a cluster or exit.
  • Timeline risk in Saudi Arabia. Tendering timelines are not fully within the company’s control and could take one to two quarters or longer; the regional situation was described as volatile.
  • Working capital and receivables risk. Government payment cycles of three to four months are structural; AR days are expected to remain relatively high.
  • ECL provisions. Quarterly provisions of 2%–2.5% of revenue are model-driven and scale with revenue.
  • Execution and strategy risk. Cited in the annual report material, alongside market competition, technological implementations, and changes in revenue, income or cash flows.
  • International nurse hiring constraint. Access to trained renal nurses is a genuine constraint on international expansion.
  • Patient adherence and awareness. Non-adherence to recommended treatment sessions, low awareness, and financial barriers in the broader industry are cited risks.
  • Macroeconomic and market risk. Macroeconomic performance in India and international markets, market preferences, and exposure to market risks are cited.
  • Capacity ceiling. Fixed capacity of three cycles per machine per day limits same-store growth; growth requires disciplined capacity addition.
  • Acquisition accounting variability. Seven Philippines acquisitions in the quarter involved goodwill split into intangibles, resulting in higher amortization that dilutes over five to seven years. Initial accounting for the seven asset transfers (aggregate consideration ₹709.30 million) is provisional, with finalization within up to twelve months under Ind AS 103.
  • Currency exposure. Currencies were largely flat this quarter, with only ₹20 lakhs favorable forex impact, but the company operates across multiple currencies.

4. Management Guidance Versus Observed Business Performance

Medium-term growth guidance. Management maintained medium-term growth guidance of 15% to 20% over the next three to five years. Observed Q1 FY27 revenue growth was 23.7% year-on-year to ₹281.8 crore, above the guided range.

Clinic addition guidance. Management has indicated intent to open 40 to 50 clinics in India every year and 10 to 15 clinics in Philippines every year, and to open a new international market every 12 to 18 months. Observed in Q1 FY27: 26 clinics added (19 in India, 7 in the Philippines), taking the network to 550 clinics across 5 countries and 357 cities (307 cities across 25 states in India). The India additions were a blend of PPP (Bihar), four private clinics, and one or two greenfields. The Philippines additions averaged 10–12 beds per center, consistent with the stated network average.

Capex guidance. Management does not provide capex guidance, only strategic-level guidance. Observed Q1 FY27 capex was ₹44 crore (versus ₹43 crore in Q1 FY26), toward center acquisitions and growth. IPO proceeds utilization stood at 68% (about ₹207 crore) of the fresh issue as of June 2026.

Margin and profitability trajectory. Adjusted EBITDA grew 30.7% to ₹65.1 crore with margin expanding 120 basis points year-on-year to 23.1% (and 220 basis points sequentially over Q4 FY26’s 20.9%). Adjusted PAT grew 41.7% to ₹36.8 crore, with margin at 13.1% versus 11.4%. Management attributed margin improvement to operating leverage and increasing share of international business, with COGS as a percentage of revenue improving 175 basis points. Annualized adjusted ROCE stood at 21%.

Volume and realization. Guests grew 13.0% to 38,262; treatments grew 13.3% to 10,31,084; RPT grew 9.2% to ₹2,733. India RPT remains at a $22–$23 price point. Network utilization was stated at 74% at the consolidated level.

Tax rate guidance. Management indicated the current ~20% tax rate is a good proxy, with India and Philippines at 25% and Uzbekistan at 0% (conditional on more than 90% of revenue from healthcare services), and the composite depending on revenue mix.

Saudi loss guidance. Management declined to guide on Saudi JV losses, stating the company is in the investment phase and visibility on tendering timelines is limited.

Depreciation. Management noted depreciation as a percentage of revenue has remained flat year-on-year at around 8.6%–8.7%, with machines depreciated over 7 to 10 years depending on country and local regulatory requirements. RFID-based fixed asset tracking reduced depreciation cost in markets like India by enabling more disciplined capital deployment — the company deployed about 50 machines per month last year for replacement and other activity, but in the last three months hardly deployed new machines, instead utilizing existing machines more efficiently. Management characterized this as a one-time benefit.

Same-store sales disclosure. Management declined to provide country-level or same-store sales splits, stating the business should be viewed as a platform story at the console level, consistent with how large global dialysis players (Fresenius and DaVita) report. Management emphasized that investments and capital allocation must be ROCE-accretive at the platform level.

Broker Narrative

The first report was a cautious Not Rated, highlighting near-term Saudi margin dilution, ~12-month greenfield breakeven, and domestic pricing/PPP constraints, while acknowledging leadership and international arbitrage. The last report shifted to Buy, replacing those concerns with an upbeat asset-light, rapid-breakeven story (greenfield in 3–4 months) and superior international unit economics. Persistent themes were India organized-dialysis leadership, international high-pricing expansion, scale economies, and supply-chain control.

Fears that came true

  • The first report’s near-term margin dilution from Saudi clinic ramp-up correlated with the -3.4% actual return (disappointment vs +23.3% Buy prediction).
  • The first report’s ~12-month greenfield gestation appeared to materialize, undermining the later 3–4 month breakeven claim and the post-Buy stock performance.
  • The first report’s upfront investment and overhead absorption delays were consistent with the negative actual return after the Buy call.

Optimism that failed

  • The last report’s optimism that greenfield setups would achieve breakeven in 3–4 months failed to materialize, as the stock returned -3.4% after the Buy call instead of the predicted +23.3%.
