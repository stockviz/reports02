Headwinds and Challenges

  • Currency pressure on product margin. India’s product margin expanded to 64.2% while “absorbing the currency headwind.” Management noted that short-term headwinds could persist if the currency worsens further, with backward integration and cost-structure work framed as an ongoing journey rather than a completed fix.
  • Supply-side constraints on store expansion. Management described supply, not demand, as the binding constraint. Serving Tier 2 and beyond requires logistics (a shift away from an air network toward an air-plus-train-plus-bus ecosystem), manpower hiring and training, and consistent customer experience. Remote optometry — now in 786 stores, up from 168 at FY25 end — was described as the key unlock that made the current Tier 2 expansion pace possible.
  • Talent and organisational agility. Asked which of product, real estate, or talent was most concerning over the medium to long term, management identified talent and culture — specifically the ability to retain speed and agility at scale, and to attract engineering talent capable of using data and AI to solve bottlenecks.
  • Conversion dilution as the funnel widens. As more first-time eye tests are conducted, conversion is expected to be lower and first-time customers may take longer to purchase. Management framed this as an inherent trade-off of market creation, requiring better CRM and queue/wait-time management.
  • NPS and policy communication. Same-store sales growth, same pin code sales growth, and NPS all moderated in the quarter. Management attributed this to confusion over certain policies communicated on social media, stated that the position had been clarified, and said NPS was “almost where it was originally” and bouncing back.
  • Densification risk. The investor question of whether same-store sales would come under stress on the journey from roughly 2,700 stores toward 10,000 stores was addressed with data: density rose from 1.5 to 1.6 stores across 1,517 existing pin codes (about 150 net new stores) over nine months, while SSSG held at about 18% and same pin code sales growth was 24%. Bangalore was cited with 189 stores, 13 net additions mostly in existing pin codes, and 20% SSSG.
  • Cyber incident. On August 20, 2026, the company disclosed a cyber incident at a sub-vendor of Lenskart Solutions Pte. Ltd. in Singapore relating to its Singapore business. The company stated there was no disruption to the company, the group company, or its customers, that the issue was promptly identified and remediated, and that the group company notified the concerned data privacy regulator in Singapore in compliance with law.
  • Premiumisation execution. Management stated it had underestimated how fast its own customers would premiumise, and separately that it took too long to crack the ₹500 price point because costs were not ready, even though the customer was.

Tailwinds and Growth Prospects

  • Market creation at scale. 78 crore Indians need vision correction today, heading toward 94 crore by FY30. Roughly 35,000 Indians walk into Lenskart stores daily and discover for the first time that they cannot see clearly. 63 lakh eye tests were conducted in India in the quarter, up 42.7%, with over 1 crore first-time eye tests in FY26.
  • Store white space. Of 6,400 pin codes mapped in the Q2 FY26 letter, 455 net new stores were added in nine months — about 5% of the white space. Over 6,100 pin codes remain unserved, densification allows for at least another 3,000 stores, and India alone points past 10,000 stores. In Q2 FY26, existing cities still had 2,821 pin codes without a single Lenskart store; 152 were entered, leaving over 2,650. 140 towns were entered this year, including Hassan, Balasore, Tura, and Gandhidham. Average revenue per store in these new towns was tracking at ₹18 lakhs per month.
  • Densification adding rather than dividing demand. Same pin code sales growth of 24% ran ahead of SSSG of 18.3%, which management read as market share gains in those markets. Visakhapatnam added 11 net stores (almost half in new pin codes) and Kolkata added 10 net stores (eight in new pin codes).
  • Premiumisation. Premium (₹10,000-plus) mix rose from 18% at DRHP filing to 20%. Owndays premium lenses are doing over ₹1,500 crores of prescription eyeglasses sales; Rodenstock and Tokai eyeglasses, with progressives priced at ₹30,000-plus, are doing roughly ₹250 crores annually. Management identified the ₹3,500–₹10,000 single-vision lens segment as largely held by global brands and a large opportunity, alongside frames above ₹5,000 (John Jacobs, Meller, Owndays) and the launch of Under Armour as an exclusive brand.
  • Value end of the market. Hustlr Club, offering ₹500 glasses with lenses and warranty included, onboarded the largest ever number of customers in “the real Bharat” in the quarter. Management stated the unit economics work and that lower price points are more margin accretive, with the model enabled by India manufacturing, logistics optimisation, and omnichannel customer acquisition measured net of acquisition cost.
  • International scale-up. International revenue grew 38% (about 29% in constant currency) with EBITDA pre-Ind AS 116 margin crossing 10% for the first time at 10.6%, up from 4.5%, with total contribution of ₹127 crores, triple the prior year. Growth was almost entirely same-store led: eyewear units up 37.6%, transacting customers up 27.8%, eye tests up 20.5%, with only 16 net new stores added. Product margin expanded to 77.1% on deeper Owndays and Meller supply chain integration. Meller, a USD 35 million brand at acquisition, is tracking to be a USD 70 million-plus brand. Management indicated acceleration plans are being made in some markets, with Thailand cited as particularly exciting, and Japan described as one of the fastest growing international markets.
  • Dual-brand strategy. In Singapore, Lenskart serves more than 25% volume market share and coexists with Owndays across different consumer segments and price points; the same strategy is being followed in Thailand, while Japan is not currently planned for a dual-brand approach.
  • Technology-led capacity expansion. Self-eye test entered pilot stores, built on one of India’s largest recorded eye test data sets. B by Lenskart is live with 80,000-plus sign-ups, manufacturing is scaling, and a few hundred glasses are shipping daily. RFID is being launched across products to reduce in-store buying time. GeoIQ is being used to build geo-analytics mapping for international markets.
  • Progressive lenses. Management described progressives as a large area where Lenskart was not historically strongest, with tech-enabled automation of marking and fitting, an in-house R&D team, AI-enabled lens design, and an Owndays adaptive customised progressive reported to have higher customer satisfaction than the most expensive lenses sold today.
  • Cash generation and returns. Operating cash flow of ₹297 crores represented 82% EBITDA-to-cash-flow conversion, sufficient to fund store capex of ₹75 crores and plant capex of ₹132 crores (largely the Hyderabad facility), leaving positive net cash inflow of ₹116 crores before M&A and equity raise. Return on capital employed improved to 23% from 14% (noted in the transcript as 14.6% in a correction).
  • Supply chain control. The board approved acquisition of an additional 19% of Baofeng Framekart Technology Limited from Mr. Geng Yongchao for approximately RMB 7.5 million (approximately ₹106 million), raising direct and indirect shareholding from 51% to 70%, expected to complete by September 30, 2026. Framekart contributes more than 30% of the Lenskart Group’s total eyewear production requirements, with turnover of RMB 99.98 million in FY25-26 (up from RMB 91.20 million and RMB 72.73 million in the prior two years). The board also approved incorporation of OWNDAYS Korea (initial investment KRW 300 million, approximately ₹20.2 million) and Wenzhou Framekart Trade Co., Ltd in China (RMB 1 million, approximately ₹14.14 million, 95% held by Framekart).
  • Brand-building initiatives. Run for Frame in Thailand drew over 40,000 runners and was extended to India with more than 5 lakh participants. Drishti ki Daud, launched with the Lenskart Foundation on Independence Day, gives a pair of glasses for 20,000 steps run and one more pair for every additional 10,000 steps.

Key Risks

  • Currency deterioration could pressure product margins despite natural hedging from non-INR revenues and structural initiatives.
  • Execution risk in scaling Tier 2 and Tier 3 operations without degrading customer experience, which management identified as the reason some brands decline.
  • Talent availability and retention, particularly engineering talent, as the primary medium-to-long-term constraint on the stated vision.
  • Potential same-store sales pressure from continued densification, though observed data so far shows SSSG holding alongside rising density.
  • Cannibalisation risk from the ₹500 price point, addressed through an engineered omnichannel journey (face scan on the app to detect duplication) and a house-of-brands positioning.
  • Accuracy and consistency of remote and AI-based eye testing, monitored through Tango Eye computer vision scoring of process steps and correlation with power-change exchanges.
  • Regulatory and data privacy exposure in international markets, as illustrated by the Singapore sub-vendor cyber incident and the notification to the Singapore data privacy regulator.
  • Dependence on the Framekart joint venture for more than 30% of group eyewear production, mitigated by the proposed increase in shareholding to 70%.
  • Seasonality in international results, with Q1 stronger due to the sunglasses peak (units up 58.4% in the quarter) and sunglasses being a higher net margin business.

Management Guidance Versus Observed Performance

  • International profitability question. Management stated that for four quarters the most common investor question was whether international would be profitable, and that “the question mark is gone.” Observed: international EBITDA pre-Ind AS 116 margin reached 10.6% in Q1 FY27 versus 4.5% a year earlier, with contribution of ₹127 crores, triple the prior year.
  • Full-year international margin. Asked whether FY27 international margin would resemble the Q1 level, management declined to guide on the full-year number relative to the quarter but said the endeavour would be to continue improving year-on-year. Observed: FY26 full-year margin was about 7%, versus 3.6% the year before, and Q1 FY27 came in at 10.6%.
  • Store addition pace. Management stated that in India it moved from 100 stores a year to 300 and now to 450-plus, and that it wants to accelerate internationally as well, while noting acceleration in international may not happen this year. Observed: 455 net new stores added in nine months in India; 116 net new stores added in India in Q1 FY27 with 50 new cities; 16 net new stores added internationally in the quarter.
  • Densification versus same-store growth. Management’s stated bet was that more stores would add demand rather than divide it. Observed: density rose from 1.5 to 1.6 in existing pin codes with SSSG at about 18% and same pin code sales growth at 24%; Q1 FY27 SSSG of 18.3% compared with 16% in the same quarter last year, which management described as an acceleration.
  • Currency offsetting. Management stated the endeavour is to keep doing backward integration and improving cost structure, while acknowledging short-term headwinds if the currency worsens. Observed: India product margin expanded to 64.2% while absorbing the currency headwind; consolidated product margin crossed 70% for the first time after four quarters at around 69%.
  • ₹500 price point economics. Management stated the unit economics work and that it was solved for before launch. Observed: the largest ever number of customers onboarded through Hustlr Club in the quarter, with management stating lower price points are more margin accretive.
  • NPS recovery. Management stated NPS is bouncing back and is almost where it was originally. Observed: NPS moderated in the quarter alongside SSSG and same pin code sales growth, attributed to policy communication confusion on social media.
  • Premiumisation. Management stated it underestimated how fast customers would premiumise and does not intend to repeat the mistake. Observed: premium mix rose from 18% at DRHP filing to 20%.
  • Headline financial performance. Revenue grew 34% year-on-year and PAT grew 182% to ₹228 crores, against full-year FY26 PAT of about ₹530 crores. India revenue grew 30.7% to ₹1,531 crores with EBITDA pre-Ind AS 116 margin of 15.4% (up from 13.3%) and EBITDA of ₹236 crores, up 51.5%. International revenue grew 38% to ₹1,203 crores. Eyewear units in India rose 22.8% to 82 lakhs.

Broker Narrative

The broker narrative began with an expansive bullish thesis centered on vertical integration, store expansion, and a structurally underpenetrated market with strong financial projections. By the last report, the themes of vertical integration and omnichannel technology persisted, but new headwinds emerged around medical infrastructure reducing eyewear demand and slower product innovation, shifting the framing from unbridled growth optimism to tempered expectations.

Fears that came true

  • Near-Term Cash Flow Pressures: Heavy upfront capex for the Hyderabad facility and rapid store expansion constrained FCF, correlating with the stock’s progressively weaker actual returns culminating in a -1.7% return on the last Buy call.

Optimism that failed

  • Market Leadership & Underpenetration: The first report’s optimism about low ~35% penetration scaling toward ~53% vision correction need was undermined by the last report’s headwind that advancement in medical infrastructure is declining demand for eyewear products such as LASIK and SMILE surgeries.
  • Technology & Scalability driving repeat purchases: The first report’s tailwind of technology enabling ~3.5 units per customer over two years was contradicted by the last report’s headwind citing slower innovation in the product portfolio and failure of newer launches.
